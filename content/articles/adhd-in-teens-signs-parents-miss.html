When people picture ADHD, they usually picture a young boy who can't sit still. That kid exists, but plenty of teens with ADHD look nothing like him. Many were fine in elementary school, then started struggling once middle and high school asked them to manage more on their own. Others have always been bright, a little scattered, and told they just need to try harder.

Signs that are easy to miss

  • Grades that don't match effort or ability. They understand the material but lose points for missing work, forgotten deadlines, or careless mistakes.
  • Homework that takes hours. Not because it's hard, but because starting and staying with it feels impossible.
  • A messy backpack, room, and phone. Organization isn't just a habit problem for these kids. It's genuinely harder for their brains.
  • Big reactions to small frustrations. ADHD affects emotional regulation, so irritability and meltdowns can be part of it.
  • Daydreaming, especially in girls. The inattentive type is quieter and often gets mistaken for shyness or anxiety.
  • Staying up late and dragging in the morning. Sleep problems and ADHD often travel together.

Why it gets missed

Smart kids can compensate for a long time. Structure at home and school can hide the problem too. When that support thins out in high school, or when a teen starts driving, working, or preparing for college, the gaps show up. Anxiety and depression can also develop on top of untreated ADHD, which makes the original issue harder to see.

What an evaluation looks like for a teen

We see teens ages 13 and up. An ADHD evaluation includes time with the teen and with a parent or guardian, a look at school history and any teacher feedback, and standard rating scales. We also check for things that can look like ADHD or show up alongside it, like anxiety, depression, and sleep problems. Teens under 18 need a parent or guardian's consent for care.

If it is ADHD

A diagnosis isn't a label to carry around. It's an explanation, and it opens the door to support that actually fits. Treatment may include medication, school accommodations, and practical routines at home. If medication is part of the plan, we follow up regularly to make sure it's working and adjust when needed. Visits can happen by telehealth anywhere in Washington, which makes it easier to fit care around school.
