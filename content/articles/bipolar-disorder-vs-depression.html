Bipolar disorder and depression can look identical on a bad day. Both can bring weeks of low mood, poor sleep, trouble concentrating, and losing interest in things you used to enjoy. The difference often isn't in the depression at all. It's in what happens at other times, and that's why it can take a while for the right diagnosis to surface.

Where the two overlap

Most people with bipolar disorder spend more time depressed than they do in any high mood. Many also come to their first appointment during a depressive episode, because that's when they feel worst and most want help. If no one asks about the other side of the pattern, it's easy to call it depression and stop there.

The depressive episodes themselves can be very similar. Some features show up a bit more often in bipolar depression, such as sleeping much more than usual, feeling physically heavy or slowed down, depression that started in the teens or early twenties, or episodes that come on quickly. None of these settle the question on their own. They're clues, not proof.

What sets bipolar disorder apart

The defining feature of bipolar disorder is a period of mania or hypomania. These are stretches of days, not a good afternoon, when mood and energy run noticeably higher than your usual self. Common signs include:

  • Needing much less sleep and not feeling tired
  • Talking faster or more than usual, or feeling like thoughts are racing
  • Starting many projects at once, or feeling unusually driven
  • Spending, driving, or making decisions in ways that are out of character
  • Feeling unusually confident, irritable, or easily set off

Full mania is usually hard to miss. It disrupts work and relationships and sometimes leads to a hospital stay. Hypomania is milder and easier to overlook. People often remember it as a stretch when they felt great and got a lot done, so they don't think to mention it. Family members and close friends often notice it before the person does.

It also helps to know that a high mood isn't always cheerful. For some people it shows up mostly as irritability, restlessness, and poor sleep, sometimes mixed in with depressive symptoms at the same time.

Why the difference matters for treatment

Depression and bipolar disorder are treated differently. For depression, an antidepressant is often a reasonable part of the plan. In bipolar disorder, an antidepressant used on its own can, in some people, set off a manic or hypomanic episode or make mood swings more frequent. Treatment for bipolar disorder usually centers on medications that keep mood stable, such as lithium or certain other mood stabilizers, along with steady sleep and daily routines.

This doesn't mean antidepressants are off the table for everyone with bipolar disorder, or that anyone currently taking one should stop. Stopping a psychiatric medication suddenly can cause its own problems. If something in this article raises a question about your own treatment, bring it to the provider who prescribes for you.

Getting the diagnosis right also shapes the longer view. Bipolar disorder is usually a long-term condition, and a plan built for it focuses on preventing future episodes, not only treating the current one.

Things worth mentioning to your provider

A careful psychiatric evaluation asks about your whole mood history, not just how you feel this month. It helps to think ahead about:

  • Any stretch of several days when you slept very little and still felt energized
  • Times others told you that you seemed "wired," different, or not yourself
  • How you responded to any antidepressant in the past, including feeling agitated or unusually up
  • Family members with bipolar disorder, depression, or a history of psychiatric hospital stays
  • Whether your moods seem to follow the seasons

Bringing a partner or family member into part of the visit, with your permission, can fill in details you might not remember. Seasonal patterns matter too.
