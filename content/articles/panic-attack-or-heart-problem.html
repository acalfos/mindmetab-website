A lot of people have their first panic attack and end up in an emergency room, sure that something is wrong with their heart. That's not an overreaction. Panic and heart trouble can feel remarkably alike, and in the moment it's hard for anyone to tell them apart without an exam.

This article explains how they overlap, what tends to set them apart, and what to do next. It isn't a way to diagnose yourself during an episode. If you're unsure, treat it as a possible emergency.

Why they feel so similar

A panic attack is a sudden surge of the body's alarm system. Adrenaline rises, the heart speeds up, breathing gets fast and shallow, and muscles tense. That chain of events can cause chest tightness, a pounding or racing heart, shortness of breath, sweating, shaking, dizziness, nausea, and tingling in the hands or face. Many people also feel a strong sense that they're dying or losing control.

A heart attack can cause chest pressure, shortness of breath, sweating, nausea, and lightheadedness too. So the list of symptoms alone doesn't settle the question.

Clues that point one way or the other

These are general patterns, not rules. Plenty of people don't fit them neatly.

  • How it builds. Panic usually rises fast and peaks within minutes, then gradually eases. Heart symptoms are more likely to linger, come and go, or get worse with exertion.
  • What the pain is like. Panic chest pain is often sharp or stabbing and may move around. Heart pain is more often described as pressure, squeezing, heaviness, or tightness in the center of the chest.
  • Where else it shows up. Pain that spreads to the arm, back, neck, jaw, or stomach is a reason to think heart first.
  • Who you are. Age, smoking, diabetes, high blood pressure, high cholesterol, and family history all raise the chance that chest symptoms are cardiac.

Heart attack symptoms can also be subtle, especially in women. Unusual fatigue, breathlessness during everyday tasks, nausea, or back and jaw pain may show up with little or no chest pain.

When to call 911

If you have chest pain or pressure, pain spreading to your arm, back, neck, or jaw, trouble breathing, a cold sweat, or fainting, call 911. Don't drive yourself. Even if you've had panic attacks before, a new or different kind of episode deserves a medical check. Getting checked and finding out your heart is fine is a good outcome, not a wasted trip.

After your heart checks out

Many people leave the ER with normal tests and no clear plan. That can be frustrating, and the fear of another attack sometimes becomes its own problem. People start avoiding driving, exercise, crowds, or anything that makes their heart beat faster.

It also helps to rule out other medical causes with your primary care provider. Thyroid problems, heart rhythm issues, low blood sugar, and too much caffeine or certain medications can all produce panic-like symptoms.

If those are ruled out and the episodes keep happening, panic is very treatable. A psychiatric evaluation looks at the pattern of your attacks, what else is going on in your life, and whether anxiety, depression, or another condition is involved.
