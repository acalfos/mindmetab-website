Once your first evaluation is done and you've started a medication, most of your care happens in follow up visits. These are shorter than the first appointment, and they have a clear purpose: checking whether the medication is helping, whether it's causing problems, and what to do next. A lot of people expect them to be rushed or routine. A good one shouldn't feel that way.

What happens in a typical visit

A medication management visit is a focused conversation. We'll ask how you've been since we last spoke, and we'll usually come back to the same handful of questions each time so we can see change over weeks and months:

  • How your main symptoms are doing compared with last time
  • Sleep, appetite, energy, and concentration
  • Any side effects, even ones that seem minor or embarrassing
  • Whether you've been able to take the medication as planned, and if not, what got in the way
  • Anything new: stress, a health change, a new prescription from another provider

Sometimes we'll use a short questionnaire for mood, anxiety, or attention. It isn't a test you can fail. It just gives us a consistent way to track how things are moving.

We also keep an eye on your physical health. Some psychiatric medications can affect weight, blood pressure, heart rate, or blood sugar, so we may ask about those or suggest labs through your primary care provider. Mental health and metabolic health are closely tied, and we pay attention to both.

How often visits happen

It depends on where you are in treatment. When you're starting a new medication or changing a dose, visits are usually closer together, because that's when side effects show up and when the dose is most likely to need adjusting. Once things are stable and working, visits are often spaced further apart.

Many medications take several weeks to show their full effect, so an early visit isn't always about deciding whether something works. Often it's about making sure you're tolerating it and that nothing concerning has come up. If something changes between visits, you don't have to wait for the next appointment to tell us.

Some medications, including stimulants for ADHD, come with extra requirements under state and federal rules. If that applies to you, we'll explain what it means for your visits. Our article on ADHD medication through telehealth covers the current rules in more detail, and our ADHD medication management page explains how we handle it.

What you can bring up

This is your time, and the most useful visits are honest ones. It helps to tell us if:

  • You stopped or skipped the medication, even for a few days
  • A side effect is bothering you more than you've let on
  • You're worried about cost, refills, or getting to the pharmacy
  • You want to try lowering the dose or coming off a medication someday
  • You're pregnant, planning a pregnancy, or breastfeeding

None of these will get you in trouble. They change what the right plan is, and we can only adjust for what we know about. It's also fine to keep a few notes on your phone between visits so you don't forget something that mattered on a hard day.

Medication decisions are shared. We'll explain why we're suggesting a change, what we expect it to do, and what to watch for. If you'd rather wait, or try something else first, say so. Please don't stop or change a medication on your own without talking to us first, since stopping some medications suddenly can cause its own problems.

What these visits don't include

Medication management isn't therapy. We don't provide therapy ourselves, but many of our patients see a therapist, and we're glad to coordinate with yours or point you toward one. Medication and therapy often work well together, and keeping everyone on the same page usually helps.

These visits also aren't emergency care. If things get much worse between appointments, contact us, and if you're ever in danger, use the crisis resources below.
